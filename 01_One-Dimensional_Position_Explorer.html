One-Dimensional Position Explorer

Position and average velocity in one dimension: use a shared coordinate system to describe where an object is and in which direction its position vector points.

Why do we need a coordinate system?

Saying “the object is over there” is not precise because different observers may use different reference points and directions. A coordinate system gives everyone the same origin, positive direction, and unit of measurement.

Position has directional information. A position to the right of the origin is different from an equally distant position to the left. A vector records both magnitude and direction.

Explore position in one dimension

Drag the orange object along the x-axis. On a keyboard, focus the object and use the arrow keys. Each small movement is 0.1 m.

Signed coordinate x = +2.0 m The sign identifies a direction from the origin.
One-component position vector r = ⟨+2.0 m⟩ General form: r = ⟨x⟩
Magnitude of position |r| = 2.0 m Magnitude is nonnegative and has no direction.
The object is 2.0 m to the right of the origin, so its position vector points in the +x direction.
Reset returns the object to x = +2.0 m.

What to notice

  • The origin is the reference point; changing sides changes the sign of x.
  • The arrow begins at the origin and points toward the object.
  • r is a vector, while |r| is its magnitude. Positions +3 m and −3 m have equal magnitudes but opposite directions.
  • At x = 0 m, the position-vector magnitude is zero, so it has no left or right direction.

Building the vector idea: 1-D → 2-D → 3-D

1-D: Motion along a line

Physical concept: One signed coordinate is enough when an object can move only left or right along a line.

Visual diagram: The explorer above shows the origin, the two possible directions, and the position-vector arrow.

Mathematical representation: r = ⟨x⟩

Simple example: If x = −3 m, then r = ⟨−3 m⟩ and |r| = 3 m.

Physical interpretation: The object is 3 m from the origin, and the negative sign says it is on the −x side.

2-D: Position on a flat surface

Physical concept: On a floor or map, left/right is not enough. We need two perpendicular directions, x and y.

Visual diagram: The vector can be understood as 3 m in +x and then 4 m in +y.

Mathematical representation: r = ⟨x, y⟩ or r = x i + y j, where i and j indicate the unit x and y directions.

Simple example: r = ⟨3, 4⟩ m. By the Pythagorean theorem, |r| = √(3² + 4²) = 5 m.

Physical interpretation: The object is 3 m right and 4 m up from the origin; its straight-line distance from the origin is 5 m.

3-D: Position in space

Physical concept: For an aircraft, drone, or object in a room, a third coordinate describes the remaining direction.

Visual diagram: The x, y, and z axes share one origin. Three components locate one point in space.

Mathematical representation: r = ⟨x, y, z⟩ or r = x i + y j + z k.

Simple example: For r = ⟨2, 3, 6⟩ m, |r| = √(2² + 3² + 6²) = √49 = 7 m.

Physical interpretation: The three components state how far to travel in each coordinate direction. The magnitude gives the straight-line distance from the origin.

Connection to average velocity

Coordinates let us describe a change in position consistently. In one dimension, suppose an object moves from xi = −2 m to xf = +4 m in 3 s.

change in position = +4 m − (−2 m) = +6 m
average velocity = ⟨+6 m ÷ 3 s⟩ = ⟨+2 m/s⟩

The positive sign means the average velocity points in the +x direction. Velocity is a vector; speed is its magnitude. For this no-reversal example, v = ⟨+2 m/s⟩ and |v| = 2 m/s.

In three dimensions, velocity may be written as:

v = ⟨vx, vy, vz⟩
v = vx i + vy j + vz k

Do not confuse velocity with speed: v includes direction, while |v| is the nonnegative magnitude called speed.

All quantities use SI units: metres (m) for position and metres per second (m/s) for velocity.
