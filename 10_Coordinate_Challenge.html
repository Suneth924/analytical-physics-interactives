Coordinate Challenge

Why coordinate systems and vectors are needed—and how a physical location becomes a consistent numerical description.

Why do we need a coordinate system?

“The object is over there” may make sense to one observer but not to another. Physics needs a shared reference so that everyone can describe the same position, direction, and motion consistently.

1

Choose a reference

Pick an origin, positive directions, labeled axes, and units. The physical point does not move when we change coordinates; only its numerical description changes.

2

Describe direction

A number alone cannot distinguish 4 m east from 4 m west. Signed components tell us how far and in which axis direction.

3

Use a vector

A vector has both magnitude and direction. Its components describe one physical quantity relative to the chosen axes.

1-D

Position along one line

Physical concept

An object on a straight track needs one signed number relative to the origin.

Visual diagram
Mathematical representation
r = <x>

In one dimension, the sign gives the direction from the origin.

Numerical example
r = <3 m>

The position coordinate is positive three metres.

Physical interpretation

The object is 3 m from the origin in the chosen positive x direction. A position of −3 m would be equally far in the opposite direction.

2-D

Position and components in a plane

Physical concept

A location on a floor needs two independent instructions: horizontal and vertical displacement from the origin.

Visual diagram
Mathematical representation
r = <x, y>

r = x i + y j

i and j indicate the positive axis directions.

Numerical example
r = <3 m, 4 m>

Magnitude: |r| = √(3² + 4²) = 5 m.

Physical interpretation

Move 3 m in +x, then 4 m in +y. The straight-line distance from the origin is 5 m. Components are not separate vectors; together they describe one position vector.

What to notice

  • The ordered pair is <x, y>: horizontal information comes first.
  • A negative component means the point lies in the negative direction of that axis; it does not mean the distance is negative.
  • Points on an axis have one zero component. The origin has two zero components.
  • The vector r and its magnitude |r| are different: r includes direction, while |r| is a nonnegative distance.
3-D

Position in three-dimensional space

Physical concept

A location in a room also needs height or depth. Three mutually independent directions require three components.

Visual diagram
Mathematical representation
r = <x, y, z>

r = x i + y j + z k
Numerical example
r = <2 m, 3 m, 6 m>

|r| = √(2² + 3² + 6²) = √49 = 7 m.

Physical interpretation

The three components give axis-by-axis instructions. The magnitude is the straight-line distance from the origin, not any single component.

Position language also prepares us for velocity

A three-dimensional velocity can be written as v = <vx, vy, vz> or v = vx i + vy j + vz k.

Velocity is a vector and includes direction. Speed is the magnitude of velocity: |v| = √(vx² + vy² + vz²). Therefore, velocity is not the same thing as speed.

Coordinate systems provide the shared language; vectors use that language to describe magnitude and direction.
