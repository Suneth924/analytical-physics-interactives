Two-Dimensional Average Velocity Explorer

One motion can involve two coordinate changes. Explore how those changes combine to form one average velocity vector.

Why do we need coordinates and vectors?

Saying “the object moved 8 meters” is incomplete if direction matters. Did it move right, left, upward, or diagonally?

A coordinate system gives everyone the same reference for describing position and motion. A vector records both magnitude and direction. Its components tell us how much of the vector points along each coordinate axis.

Building from one dimension to three

At every stage: begin with the physical idea, draw it, represent it mathematically, calculate a simple example, and interpret the result.

1-D: one axis

Motion along a line

  1. Physical concept: a position is left or right of an origin.
  2. Visual: a number line supplies an origin and a positive direction.
  3. Mathematics: r = <x>.
  4. Example: r = <4 m>.
  5. Interpretation: the object is 4 m in the positive x direction.
2-D: two axes

Motion on a plane

  1. Physical concept: a position can be horizontal and vertical.
  2. Visual: perpendicular x and y axes share one origin.
  3. Mathematics: r = <x,y> = x i + y j.
  4. Example: r = <3 m,2 m>.
  5. Interpretation: move 3 m in +x and 2 m in +y to reach the position.
3-D: three axes

Motion in space

  1. Physical concept: real locations can also differ in height or depth.
  2. Visual: add a z axis perpendicular to x and y.
  3. Mathematics: r = <x,y,z> = x i + y j + z k.
  4. Example: r = <3 m,2 m,4 m>.
  5. Interpretation: all three components are needed to locate the object in space.

From the physical change to the algebra

First compare the final coordinates with the initial coordinates:

Δx = xf − xi    and    Δy = yf − yi

The two changes form one displacement vector:

Δr = <Δx, Δy>

Divide both displacement components by the same elapsed time:

v = <vx,vy> = <Δx/Δt, Δy/Δt> = vx i + vy j

The magnitude follows from the Pythagorean theorem:

|v| = √(vx2 + vy2)
Vector versus magnitude: v is a velocity vector, so it includes direction. |v| is its magnitude, a nonnegative number. The magnitude of velocity is the average speed for the straight, constant-rate motion shown here; velocity and speed are not the same kind of quantity.

In three dimensions the same idea becomes v = <vx,vy,vz> = vx i + vy j + vz k.

What to notice

  • There is one two-dimensional average velocity vector, not two separate motions.
  • Its horizontal and vertical components come from two coordinate changes over the same elapsed time.
  • A negative component indicates direction along a negative axis; it does not mean a negative magnitude.
  • If Δx is zero, the velocity is vertical. If Δy is zero, it is horizontal. If both are zero, the average velocity is zero.
  • Increasing the elapsed time while keeping both positions fixed decreases both velocity components and the magnitude.
