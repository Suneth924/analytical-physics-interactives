Why do we need coordinates and vectors?

Saying that an object is “five meters away” is not enough to locate it. It might be five meters to the right, above, behind, or in some other direction. Physical quantities such as position and velocity can include direction, so everyone needs a common reference.

A coordinate system is a shared reference. It specifies an origin, positive directions, and units so different observers can describe the same location consistently.
A vector has magnitude and direction. Its components tell us how much of the vector points along each coordinate direction.
The view is not the vector. Rotating a diagram changes its appearance, but it does not change the point's coordinates in the fixed coordinate system.

Physical concept: Motion is restricted to one straight line. One signed coordinate is enough.

r = <x>

Example: r = <3> m.

Physical interpretation: The object is 3 m from the origin in the positive x direction. The sign carries directional information.

  2. Diagram

Physical concept: On a floor or map, one coordinate describes left–right and another describes down–up.

r = <x,y> = x i + y j

Example: r = <3,4> m, so |r| = √(3² + 4²) = 5 m.

Physical interpretation: Travel 3 m along +x and 4 m along +y to reach the point. The vector's magnitude is the straight-line distance from the origin.

Physical concept: A room requires a third independent direction. We use +x to the right, +y upward, and +z out of the page toward the viewer in the front view.

r = <x,y,z> = x i + y j + z k

The symbols i, j, k represent one unit in the positive x, y, and z directions.

Example: r = <3,2,4> m.

Physical interpretation: The three components are instructions measured from the origin. They belong to the coordinate system and do not depend on the viewing angle.

What to notice

  • Rotate the display without moving the sliders. The diagram changes, but <x,y,z> does not.
  • Each component describes a separate direction. Changing x alone moves P parallel to the x axis.
  • The projection lines connect the 3-D point to the coordinate planes and show how its three coordinates are constructed.
  • The vector r is not the same as its magnitude |r|. The vector has direction; its magnitude is one nonnegative number.

Position and velocity in three dimensions

Velocity is also a vector

Velocity states both how fast and in what direction an object moves. Its three components describe motion along the coordinate directions:

v = <vx,vy,vz>
v = vx i + vy j + vz k

A negative component means motion in the negative direction of that axis; it does not mean “negative speed.”

Simple numerical example

Suppose v = <2,−1,2> m/s. The object moves in +x, −y, and +z at the same time.

|v| = √(2² + (−1)² + 2²) = √9 = 3 m/s
Velocity versus speed: v is the velocity vector and includes direction. |v| is its magnitude, which is the speed.
Use the coordinate system as a physical reference—not merely as a set of equations to memorize.
